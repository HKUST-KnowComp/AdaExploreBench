Your job is to discover as many usable three-field lexicographic groups and to author the defensible first-to-last priority rules for those groups. Treat every supplied field as a candidate, but do not force a field into an invalid group merely to increase coverage or count.

A lexicographic group is a set of three distinct fields that can serve as successive decision cues for the same scenario, decision objective, and action ladder. Each field must have a realistic, nonempty region in which its value can independently settle an action or intervention level without being numerically combined with the other two; Each field must also have a meaningful indifference or inconclusive region in which a later cue can still provide residual decision evidence. In this way, the three fields [A,B,C] could form a priority rule: If A satisfy a condition, then it directly decides the final decision, no matter what values B and C take; Else if B satisfy a condition, then it decides the final decision regardless of C; Else we make the final decision based on the value of C. The three fields should not be aliases, deterministic restatements, fields whose decision meaning depends on jointly calculating another member, mutually exclusive measurements with no realistic common decision condition, and sets for which no member can leave a meaningful unresolved case to the next cue. A valid group needs at least one coherent ordering, but its priority need not be inherent, universal, or the only defensible policy choice.

For example, [current_collateral_shortfall_usd, settlement_failure_probability, minutes_to_settlement_cutoff] can form a valid lexicographic group: a material collateral shortfall may independently settle the intervention level; when the shortfall is within its indifference region, settlement-failure probability provides a distinct risk cue; and when both are inconclusive, time to cutoff supplies the final urgency cue. Likewise, [vehicle_range_remaining_km, driver_duty_time_remaining_minutes, shift_time_remaining_minutes] can form a valid group: critically insufficient vehicle range may require recovery on physical-feasibility grounds; when range is not decisive, insufficient legal duty time may independently require reassignment; and when neither constraint settles the case, remaining shift time provides the final operational-horizon cue. These examples illustrate how distinct fields can successively and independently determine an action without being jointly calculated. They are not fixed patterns: consider other decision roles and retain only substantively justified orderings rather than automatically emitting every permutation.

Your task is to find as many distinct valid three-field groups as the catalog meaningfully supports, then determine which exact orderings of each group have a scenario-grounded rationale. In an ordered rule [first, second, third], the first field settles the decision whenever it is materially decisive and otherwise passes the case to the second; the second does the same for the cases left unresolved by the first; the third supplies the final cue when both earlier fields are inconclusive. Notice that we do not require the ordering to be concrete or very obvious, because the order is a rule to be established, we only require the group to be reasonable and not counterintuitive. Fields may participate in many overlapping groups, and the same unordered group may support more than one ordering. Do not partition the catalog into disjoint triples, stop after a convenient number, or emit all six permutations by default without fully analyzing. Do not set up groups purely based on the fields' name, you should think based on their description and actual role.

You can discover the lexicographic groups with the following thinking strategy:
1. Read every field's description and identify the decision role it can independently play, such as current state or severity, consequence magnitude, likelihood, urgency or time horizon, trend or persistence, recoverability, evidence reliability, or execution feasibility. Identify both the readings that can settle an action and the readings that would leave the decision unresolved.
2. Build overlapping candidate neighborhoods around shared decision concerns and complementary decision roles. A field may belong to several neighborhoods. Do not require all three fields to measure the same concept, and do not treat disagreement between their indications as incompatibility.
3. Within each neighborhood, identify strong anchor pairs: two nonredundant fields that can each independently settle some cases and can sensibly be consulted in succession without requiring a joint calculation.
4. Expand each anchor pair with every substantively distinct third field that can resolve cases left inconclusive by the pair. Revisit a strong pair with alternative third cues instead of accepting only the first plausible completion.
5. Revisit fields that are absent or rarely used. Search for additional neighborhoods, anchor pairs, and third-cue roles that connect them to the rest of the catalog, but do not force every field to belong to a lexicographic group
6. Deduplicate the resulting unordered groups. For each retained group, compare the plausible first, second, and third assignments and keep only the orderings whose three positions have distinct, concrete policy roles. Similar wording with the positions permuted is not a sufficient rationale.
7. Make a final coverage sweep across decision roles, semantic neighborhoods, reused anchor pairs, and underused fields so that obvious valid groups are not omitted.

For every retained ordered rule, output exactly three distinct supplied field IDs in first-to-last order, a stable and unique order_id that distinguishes alternative orders, and one concise rationale tied to that exact sequence. Return the supported ordered triples in the required flat output format; do not output intermediate neighborhoods, anchor pairs, unordered-group records, quotas, or unsupported permutations.
